JPG, PNG, WEBP, AVIF, GIF or SVG? A Guide to Web Image Formats

Images are usually the heaviest part of a web page. Choosing the right format can halve page weight while making graphics sharper. Here is a practical guide to the six formats you will meet most often, and when to use each.

JPG (JPEG)

The classic format for photographs, around since 1992.

  • Strengths: universal support, good compression for photos, adjustable quality.
  • Weaknesses: lossy compression creates artefacts around sharp edges and text; no transparency.
  • Use for: photos when you need maximum compatibility, such as email or older systems.

PNG

A lossless format that supports full transparency.

  • Strengths: pixel-perfect quality, crisp edges, transparency.
  • Weaknesses: large files for photos.
  • Use for: screenshots, images with text, graphics with transparency when SVG is not an option.

WEBP

Developed by Google, WEBP supports both lossy and lossless compression, transparency and animation.

  • Strengths: typically 25–35% smaller than JPG or PNG at similar quality; supported by all modern browsers.
  • Weaknesses: some older software and desktop apps still do not open it.
  • Use for: the default format for photos and complex images on modern websites.

AVIF

Based on the AV1 video codec, AVIF is the newest widely supported format.

  • Strengths: often smaller than WEBP at the same quality, supports transparency and HDR.
  • Weaknesses: slower to encode; support in older browsers and editing tools is still catching up.
  • Use for: large hero images and photo-heavy sites, with a WEBP or JPG fallback.

GIF

A 1987 format limited to 256 colours, still famous for short animations.

  • Strengths: universal animation support.
  • Weaknesses: poor quality, large files and only on/off transparency.
  • Use for: rarely. Use MP4/WEBM video or animated WEBP for animations, and SVG/CSS animation for simple motion graphics.

SVG

A vector format: shapes instead of pixels.

  • Strengths: perfectly sharp at any size, tiny files for simple graphics, styleable with CSS and animatable.
  • Weaknesses: not suitable for photos; complex illustrations can become large.
  • Use for: logos, icons, illustrations, charts, diagrams and UI graphics.

Quick decision guide

ImageBest formatFallback
Logo or iconSVGPNG
PhotoAVIF or WEBPJPG
Screenshot with textPNG or lossless WEBPPNG
Illustration with flat coloursSVGPNG or WEBP
Short animationVideo (MP4/WEBM) or SVG/CSSAnimated WEBP or GIF

Serving modern formats with fallbacks

<picture>
  <source srcset="hero.avif" type="image/avif">
  <source srcset="hero.webp" type="image/webp">
  <img src="hero.jpg" alt="Workspace with laptop" width="1200" height="800">
</picture>

The browser picks the first format it supports. Always include width, height and alt text.

Other performance tips

  • Resize images to the largest size they will be displayed at (×2 for high-density screens).
  • Use loading="lazy" on images below the fold.
  • Use srcset and sizes to serve smaller files to phones.
  • Optimise SVGs and enable compression on your server.

Summary

Use SVG for graphics, AVIF or WEBP for photos with JPG as a fallback, PNG for pixel-perfect screenshots, and video instead of GIF. Matching format to content is one of the easiest ways to make a website faster and sharper.
