It happens all the time: a business needs its logo for a sign, a T-shirt or a new website, but the only copy anyone can find is a small PNG pulled from an old email. The original vector file is gone, and the designer who made it is unreachable. Adobe Illustrator's Image Trace is the classic fix — but it costs a subscription. The good news is that you can recreate a clean SVG version in minutes with free tools. Here is the process we recommend.

What does it mean to vectorize a logo?

Vectorizing a logo means converting a pixel image of it (PNG or JPG) into a vector file (SVG, PDF or EPS) made of editable shapes that stay sharp at any size. The result can be printed on a billboard, cut on a vinyl cutter, embroidered or used as a crisp website icon — none of which works well with a small raster file.

What you need

  • The best available image of the logo — PNG preferred.
  • A free tracer: our Logo to SVG converter runs in your browser with no signup.
  • Optional: a free vector editor such as Inkscape or Figma for final touch-ups.
  • Your brand colour codes (hex values), if you have them.

Step 1: Find the best source image

Tracing quality depends mostly on the input, so start with the largest, sharpest copy of the logo you can find. Look for at least 500 pixels wide, ideally 1,000 or more. Check old emails, website media libraries, social profile images (download the full-size version), letterhead templates and PDFs — a PDF may even contain the original vector. Avoid screenshots of screenshots and heavily compressed JPGs, which create blotchy artefacts around edges. A PNG with a transparent background is ideal.

Step 2: Trace it

Open the Logo to SVG converter and drop the image into the upload area, or paste it with Ctrl+V. The tool analyses the image and selects the Logo / Clip art type for flat-colour graphics. This preset limits the number of colours and keeps corners sharp.

Step 3: Get the colours right

Set the number of colours to the number of colours in the logo, then replace each with your exact brand hex code. If you see extra in-between shades in the palette, choose a simpler option from Suggested palettes — for example the 2- or 3-colour version. Then click each swatch and enter the official hex value. If the logo sits on a white box you do not want, click the × on the white swatch to make it transparent.

Step 4: Fine-tune detail and corners

Open Advanced settings if you need more control:

Step 5: Download and review

Click Download SVG. Open the file in Figma, Inkscape or any vector editor and check that each colour sits on its own layer and that there are no leftover fragments around the edges.

Step 6: Perfect the lettering (optional)

For the most professional result, replace traced text with real type in the logo's original font. Tracing reproduces most lettering well, but tiny or thin letters from a low-resolution source can look slightly soft. Identify the font with a tool such as WhatTheFont, type the text in Inkscape or Figma, match the size and spacing over the traced version, convert it to outlines and delete the traced letters. Simple geometric elements — circles, rectangles, stars — can be redrawn the same way for perfectly clean shapes. Our guide to cleaning up a traced SVG covers more techniques.

Common problems and fixes

  • Jagged edges — use a larger source image or raise noise smoothing by one step.
  • Too many colours — pick a suggested palette with fewer colours.
  • Missing thin lines — raise Detail and lower Speckle removal.
  • White halo around shapes — make the white colour transparent or reduce the colour count.
  • Gradients look banded — tracing produces flat colours; replace the bands with a real gradient in a vector editor.

What to do with your new vector logo

Save the SVG as your master file, then export a PDF for printers and PNGs for platforms that need them. Use our SVG to PNG converter to create transparent PNGs at several sizes. For a complete set of files, see Logo File Formats Every Business Should Have.

How do you vectorize a logo from a low-resolution image?

Enlarge the image two to three times with a good upscaler before tracing, use High quality and a higher detail setting, then rebuild any soft text by hand. A 150-pixel logo simply does not contain enough information for perfect curves. Upscaling gives the tracer more room to place smooth edges, and the converter automatically works at a higher resolution for non-photo images. Small text is the part most likely to need manual rebuilding with the original font, so budget a little extra time for it.

Should you redraw the logo instead?

Redraw the logo when it is very simple, very low-resolution or will be used at huge sizes such as building signage. A logo made of a few geometric shapes and a typeface can often be rebuilt from scratch in Figma or Inkscape in less time than cleaning up a poor trace, and the result is mathematically perfect. For organic, illustrated or hand-drawn logos, tracing preserves character that is hard to reproduce manually.

Frequently asked questions

Can I vectorize a logo for free?

Yes. Browser-based tracing and free editors like Inkscape and Figma cover the whole process without paying for Illustrator.

How long does it take?

The tracing itself takes seconds. Adjusting colours takes a minute or two, and optional lettering clean-up may take 10–20 minutes for a text-heavy logo.

Will the vectorized logo be identical to the original?

Very close for flat-colour logos from a good source. Tracing is an approximation, so tiny details from low-resolution images may differ slightly; rebuilding text and simple shapes closes the gap.

Is it legal to vectorize a logo?

Yes for your own logo or one you are authorised to work on. Vectorizing someone else's trademark does not give you rights to use it.

Summary

With a good source image, a free tracer and a few minutes of colour and detail adjustment, you can recover a scalable, editable logo without Illustrator — ready for signs, merchandise, print and the web.
